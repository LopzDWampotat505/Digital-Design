\section{Introduction}\label{Intro}  
Digital electronics are the backbone of the technological society we live in today. This advancement has provided humanity with multiple benefits,
ranging from comunications to health care. The evolution of digital electronics has been marked by the devopment of numerous technologies
and methodologies that have revolutionized the way we process and transmit information. One of these improvements are the development of 
combinational logic circuits, which are fundamental components in digital systems. These circuits perform specific logical operations based on the combination of input signals,
providng the users with quick and efficient data processing capabilities, whithout the need for memory elements. Through the development of 
combinational circuits in the laboratory, we are able to understand and develop a deep understanding of the applications of digital electronics in various fields, such as computing, telecommunications, and control systems.

\section{Objectives}\label{Objectives}  
\begin{itemize}
  \item Design combinational logic circuits.
  \item Program and simulate custom combinational logic circuits in VHDL.
\end{itemize}
To achieve the objectives mentioned above, we must first understand the concepts of combinational logic circuits and the implementation in VHDL programming.
First and foremost, combinational logic circuits "are based on present inputs, and produces outputs that directly corresponds to the applied input signals." \cite{Joseph2025Hardware}. 
This means that the output of the circuit is determined solely by the current state of the inputs, without any memory or feedback elements involved.

\section{Methodology}\label{Meth}
The methodology applied in this laboratory practice was structured into three main phases: logical analysis, simulation in EDA Playground, and physical implementation on the Basys 3 board.

\subsection{Logical Analysis Phase}
During this first stage, the following activities were carried out:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The truth tables and/or the functional requirements for each problem were analyzed and defined.
    \item Based on the truth tables or logical functions, the logic for the solution was laid out and optimized.
    \item Finally, the obtained results were documented for use in the subsequent simulation and programming phases.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{EDA Playground Simulation Phase}
In the EDA Playground digital simulation environment, the following procedures were performed:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The rock, paper and scissors game, water pump program, and coin detector circuits were programmed using the VHDL language.
    \item Testbenches were developed to verify the behavior of each circuit to prove certain behaviours of the code.
    \item The results obtained from the simulations were analyzed and compared with the previously developed truth tables and logic functions, ensuring the correct logical implementation of each design.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Basys 3 Board Implementation Phase}
Finally, the physical implementation of the circuits was carried out on the Basys 3 board, following the steps described below:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The code for each circuit was synthesized, and the corresponding bitstream files were generated.
    \item Pin constraints (\texttt{.xdc} file) were assigned to map the logical signals to the physical components of the board (switches, LEDs, and displays).
    \item The designs were loaded onto the Basys 3 board, verifying their correct operation through practical input and output tests.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Water Pump}\label{Cisterna}
For the water pump program, the objective was to design a combinational system capable of controlling the filling of a water tank through a
hydraulic system composed of a tank, a motor, and a cistern.
The system has four level sensors, two called A and B, corresponding to the water tank’s
high and low levels. Another two, C and D, correspond to the high and low levels of the
cistern.
The sensors are equal to zero when no liquid is present and equal to one when there is.
An output M is required to activate the motor of a pump, with which the water will be
taken from the cistern to the tank.
The system should turn on the motor (M = 1) only when the cistern is not empty and the
tank is not full of water. For this circuit, the simplified boolean expression obtained was:\\
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{M} = \neg A \cdot D
\end{equation}

\section{Analysis of Results}\label{Analysis}
In this laboratory practice, three combinational logic circuits were designed and verified: a Rock Paper Scissors game, a Water Pump controller, and a Coin Detector. The analysis followed the three main phases outlined in the methodology: logical analysis based on functional requirements and truth tables, VHDL simulation in EDA Playground, and physical implementation and testing on the Basys 3 board. The observed behavior in each phase is described in detail below.

\subsection{Rock Paper Scissors Game}
\subsubsection{Logical Analysis}
The logic for the game was defined by five inputs: a "play" button (\texttt{PB}) and two-bit inputs for Player A (\texttt{JAA}, \texttt{JAB}) and Player B (\texttt{JBA}, \texttt{JBB}). The system generates three mutually exclusive outputs: Player A wins (\texttt{GJA}), Player B wins (\texttt{GJB}), and Tie (\texttt{E}). As detailed in the truth tables (Tables \ref{tab:vhdl_juego_inputs} and \ref{tab:vhdl_juego_outputs}), the outputs are only processed when the \texttt{PB} input is high. The logic correctly maps the game's rules: \texttt{GJA} is '1' when Player A's combination beats Player B's (e.g., Paper vs. Rock), \texttt{GJB} is '1' for the reverse, and \texttt{E} is '1' when both player inputs are identical (a draw). When \texttt{PB} is low, all outputs remain '0'.

\subsubsection{EDA Playground Analysis}
The simulation waveforms, shown in Figure \ref{fig:lab_ppt_eda}, were analyzed using the testbench from Listing \ref{lst:vhdl_tb_juego}. The testbench systematically cycled through various input combinations. The output vector (\texttt{SAL}) responded exactly as predicted by the truth tables. For instance, when Player A and Player B inputs were identical and \texttt{PB} was high, the \texttt{E} (tie) bit activated. When Player A's input represented a winning move against Player B, \texttt{GJA} activated. The transitions were clean and occurred without interference, validating the VHDL logic in Listing \ref{lst:vhdl_juego}.

\subsubsection{Basys 3 Implementation}
On the Basys 3 board, switches were employed to represent the player inputs and the \texttt{PB} signal. Three distinct LEDs were assigned to the \texttt{GJA}, \texttt{GJB}, and \texttt{E} outputs. Physical testing confirmed the simulation results perfectly. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:AWIN}, setting Player A to a winning combination and activating 'play' illuminated the \texttt{GJA} LED. Figure \ref{fig:bwin} demonstrates a win for Player B, and Figure \ref{fig:tie} shows the \texttt{E} LED active during a draw. The physical behavior was immediate and fully matched the theoretical design.

\subsection{Water Pump}
\subsubsection{Logical Analysis}
The water pump system is controlled by four sensors: tank high (\texttt{A}), tank low (\texttt{B}), cistern high (\texttt{C}), and cistern low (\texttt{D}). A single output \texttt{M} activates the motor. The primary requirement is to activate the motor (\texttt{M = 1}) only when the cistern is not empty AND the tank is not full. The "cistern not empty" condition is met when sensor \texttt{D} is active (\texttt{D=1}), as it detects water at the low level. The "tank not full" condition is met when sensor \texttt{A} is inactive (\texttt{A=0}). This leads to the simplified Boolean expression $\mathbf{M} = \neg A \cdot D$, as shown in Equation (1). This demonstrates that the states of sensors \texttt{B} and \texttt{C} are "don't care" conditions for the motor's activation logic.

\subsubsection{EDA Playground Analysis}
The simulation in EDA Playground (Figure \ref{fig:lab_cis_eda}) was performed using the VHDL code that directly implements the simplified equation $\mathbf{M} = \neg A \cdot D$. The testbench (Listing \ref{lst:vhdl_tb_juego}) iterated through all 16 possible states of the four sensors. The resulting waveforms confirmed that the output \texttt{M} was high *only* during the time intervals when \texttt{A='0'} and \texttt{D='1'}. For all other 14 combinations (e.g., \texttt{A='1'} or \texttt{D='0'}), the motor output \texttt{M} remained low, precisely validating the VHDL implementation.

\subsubsection{Basys 3 Implementation}
For physical implementation, four switches represented sensors \texttt{A}, \texttt{B}, \texttt{C}, and \texttt{D}, while a single LED represented the motor \texttt{M}. The physical tests corroborated the logical analysis. Figures \ref{fig:CISCOND1} and \ref{fig:CISCOND2} show the two valid "ON" conditions ($\neg A \cdot D$), where the \texttt{M} LED is illuminated. Figure \ref{fig:CISOFF} shows one of the many "OFF" conditions (in this case, \texttt{A='1'}, representing a full tank). The immediate response of the LED to the switch positions was in complete agreement with the simulation and the derived Boolean function.

\subsection{Coin detector}
\subsubsection{Logical Analysis}
The coin detector identifies three valid coin types based on inputs from three photocells: \texttt{A}, \texttt{B}, and \texttt{C}. The system outputs indicate the specific coin or an error state. As per the problem description, a \$1 coin activates only \texttt{C} (input "001"). A \$5 coin activates \texttt{B} and \texttt{C} (input "011"). A \$10 coin activates all three, \texttt{A}, \texttt{B}, and \texttt{C} (input "111"). The "000" input signifies no coin. Any other combination (e.g., "100", "010", "110") is an invalid or unexpected physical state and triggers the error output (defined as "111" in the VHDL code). This logic ensures that only physically possible combinations of sensors are recognized as valid coins.

\subsubsection{EDA Playground Analysis}
The simulation waveforms in Figure \ref{fig:lab_mon_eda} verify the correct logical behavior. The testbench cycled through all 8 possible input combinations for \texttt{A}, \texttt{B}, and \texttt{C}. As expected, the input "001" (representing the \$1 coin) produced the corresponding unique output ("100"). The input "011" (\$5 coin) and "111" (\$10 coin) likewise triggered their unique outputs ("010" and "001", respectively). Crucially, all other invalid combinations, such as "100", correctly activated the error state ("111"), validating the robustness of the VHDL design against unexpected inputs.

\subsubsection{Basys 3 Implementation}
Physical implementation on the Basys 3 board used three switches for inputs \texttt{A}, \texttt{B}, and \texttt{C}, and three LEDs to display the output \texttt{MON}. The results precisely mirrored the simulation. Figure \ref{fig:1_coin} shows the \$1 coin state (input "001") activating the first LED. Figure \ref{fig:5_coin} shows the \$5 coin state (input "011") activating the second LED. Figure \ref{fig:10_coin} shows the \$10 coin state (input "111") activating the third LED. Furthermore, Figure \ref{fig:ERROR} demonstrates an invalid input combination ("010") correctly activating all three LEDs, signaling the error condition. This complete agreement between the physical test and the simulation confirms a successful synthesis.

It is now safe to say that the goals were met after this practice was developed.  A structured method that included logical analysis, VHDL simulation, and physical testing on the Basys 3 board was used to successfully design and build combinational logic circuits. 
 The truth tables, simulation waveforms, and real-world operation all showed that each circuit (the Rock Paper Scissors game, the Water Pump controller, and the Coin Detector) worked properly.
